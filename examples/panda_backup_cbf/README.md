# Panda backup-trajectory CBF safety filter: real time at batch 256 with cusadi2, not with frax

A predictive (backup-trajectory) control-barrier-function safety filter for the Franka Panda,
run for 256 robots at once on one RTX 4080 at a 50 Hz control rate (20 ms budget). The model
side is evaluated either by [frax](https://github.com/danielpmorton/frax) + cbfpy (JAX) or by
cusadi2 (CasADi graph compiled to CUDA), and the QP is solved either by qpax (JAX) or by a
batched CUDA interior-point solver written here. Three pipelines are compared:

| pipeline | model side | QP solve |
|---|---|---|
| frax + qpax | frax / cbfpy (JAX), rollout in JAX | qpax (vmapped PDIP) |
| frax + CUDA IPM | frax / cbfpy (JAX), rollout in JAX | CUDA interior point (this directory) |
| cusadi2 + CUDA IPM | cusadi2 staged kernels | CUDA interior point (this directory) |

The second row is the fairness row: frax with the same solver underneath, so that the claim
rests on model evaluation and not on qpax. Measured results, the pass test and the closed-loop
check are in [`results/report.md`](results/report.md) (generated by `report.py`), the plot in
`results/bench.png`, the video in `results/closed_loop_b256_N20.mp4`.

Headline (RTX 4080, fp64, batch 256, N = 20, 1358 QP rows, median per tick; budget 20 ms):

| pipeline | model | solve | end to end (p99) | × budget |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| frax + qpax | 35.0 ms | 27.4 ms | 61.8 ms (63.6) | 3.1 |
| frax + CUDA IPM | 35.0 ms | 6.9 ms | 41.6 ms (42.3) | 2.1 |
| cusadi2 + CUDA IPM | 6.2 ms | 6.9 ms | 13.2 ms (13.5) | 0.66 |

The pass test fixed before the runs (ours p99 ≤ 16 ms; frax's model side alone ≥ 30 ms) holds.
In closed loop (256 simulated Pandas, 20 s at 50 Hz) the applied pipeline takes 12.9 ms per
tick (p99 13.3, max 14.7), frax + qpax evaluated on the same states takes 56.6 ms, every barrier
stays above −0.006 (solver-tolerance transients, as in the one-step filter), and the predictive
rows change the applied torque in 8 % of instance-ticks (up to 14 % of the batch in one tick).
Only at batches 4 to 64 does the frax model fit the budget at all, and only with our solver
under it; at batch 1 the frax pipelines are also over budget (24 ms model side, XLA launch-bound).

## The method

The one-step filter of the profiling (`profiling/`, frax's OSCBF demo: joint limits,
singularity, one spherical obstacle, self-collision, floor; 64 barriers, 78 QP rows) is
extended with a backup rollout. At every tick the state is rolled forward N = 20 steps of
20 ms under a backup brake

    u_b(z) = clip(g(q) − M(q) v / τ, −u_max, u_max),   τ = 0.1 s

(gravity compensation plus inertia-shaped damping, semi-implicit Euler), and every barrier at
every predicted state, H_k(z) = h₂(φ_k(z)), becomes a relative-degree-1 constraint on the
current torque through the flow:

    −L_g H_k · u ≤ α H_k + L_f H_k,   L_f H_k = ∂H_k/∂z f(z),  L_g H_k = ∂H_k/∂z g(z),  α = 10.

The Lie derivatives need only the 8 tangents [f(z), g(z)] pushed through the rollout, not a
full sensitivity. The k = 0 rows are cbfpy's relative-degree-2 rows unchanged, the torque
bounds are unchanged, so at N = 0 the QP is the verified one-step filter. Rows: 78 + 64 N =
1358. The QP is cbfpy's l1-relaxed CBF-QP (penalties 1e3 on CBF rows, 1e5 on the bounds,
qpax tolerance 1e-5, 30 iterations) in every pipeline.

Why this formulation (decisions from the design interview, 2026-10-08): the horizon multiplies
the model work, which is where cusadi2's advantage was measured in `profiling/`, while the QP
stays 7 variables; the backup-trajectory form (Gurriet, Ames et al.) rather than an MPC-style
predictive filter keeps the solver dense and small (no cuDSS, whose batched path costs 3.4 ms
per solve at batch 256 in `examples/humanoid_mit/mpc`); the Panda rather than the G1 because
58 tangents through a 29 × 29 mass-matrix solve over the horizon would not fit the budget on
our side either; N chosen by measurement (largest horizon that leaves our pipeline under 16 ms
p99 at batch 256 and makes frax's model side alone exceed 30 ms); τ = 0.1 s so the arm is at
rest within the horizon (velocity below 2 % of its start after 20 steps; a constant per-joint
damping is unstable at 20 ms on the wrist joints, a tanh saturation lets the arm sag under
under-compensated gravity, hence the clip).

| `cbf_ipm.cu`, `ipm.py`, `validate_ipm.py` | the CUDA interior-point solver, its JAX front end, validation and timing against qpax |
| `bench_frax_variants.py`, `time_staged.py` | frax implementation variants (fastest reported), bare cusadi2 kernel times |
| `bench.py`, `report.py` | the three-pipeline sweep over batch 1 … 4096 and the report |
| `closed_loop.py` | 256 simulated Pandas (MuJoCo, frax's demo scene) at 50 Hz under the cusadi2 + CUDA IPM pipeline, with video |
| `fp32_check.py` | accuracy of the frax model in float32 (TF32 and non-TF32) |

Build and run order: `verify.py` → `build_staged.py` → `time_staged.py` → `bench_frax_variants.py`
→ `validate_ipm.py --time` → `bench.py` → `closed_loop.py` → `report.py`. Everything builds into
`build/double/` on first use. frax and cbfpy are the unmodified versions of `profiling/`
(`profiling/third_party/frax` at the pinned commit, pip cbfpy 0.1.0 / qpax 0.1.4).

## Why staged kernels

A single CasADi function for the whole rollout is the wrong shape for cusadi2's level kernel:
its ASAP schedule keeps too many values live. At N = 8 it fits (504k nodes, 1426 levels,
11k live slots), at N = 12 it fits only by windowing into 24k levels, at N = 16 it does not fit
shared memory at all. The staged form has a head plan (37k nodes, 285 levels, 31 KB) and a
step plan (62k nodes, 228 levels, 36 KB) launched N times from one FFI call, with the state
and its 8 tangents ping-ponging through two device buffers. Plan size is then independent of
N and the horizon is a runtime loop count. At batch 256 the head costs 0.32 ms and each step
0.29 ms (bare kernel, CUDA events), against about 1.7 ms per step for the frax rollout.

## Fairness notes

- The frax column uses frax and cbfpy as published, called the way a competent JAX user would:
  one mass-matrix solve per step shared by the policy and the tangents, the 8 tangents as one
  batched jvp, everything under one jit. Four implementation variants are timed and the fastest
  is reported (the unrolled loop with vmapped jvp; jacfwd on a basis is 1.8× slower).
- frax's dynamics and collision code are not modified.
- The CUDA solver drops rows that provably cannot be active inside the torque box (exact for
  the box-constrained QP); qpax solves all rows. Timings with screening off are in
  `results/report.md` (solver 8.1 ms instead of 7.0 ms at batch 256).
- fp32: the frax model in float32 with XLA's default TF32 matmuls is numerically unusable after
  20 steps of sensitivities (50 % error in the rows); with TF32 disabled
  (`JAX_DEFAULT_MATMUL_PRECISION=highest`) it is accurate to 5e-6 and costs 9.0 ms at batch 256,
  the same as with TF32. qpax does not converge in fp32 on this QP, so the published stack has
  no fp32 path, but frax's fp32 model feeding our fp64 solver would sum to about 15.9 ms, inside
  the 20 ms budget. **The claim therefore holds in fp64 and not in fp32**; see the footnote in
  `results/report.md`. A float32 cusadi2 build was not run.
- Timing: one jitted program per pipeline, batch on the device, synchronous calls, median and
  p99, same 4096-state dataset (`profiling/sampling.py`) for every pipeline.
